A win is a clear possession level event physicality victory — verticality on a shot at the rim, ball pressure leading to a turnover, drawing a foul getting free of a hold. A microwin is smaller: blowing up a dho, a hard cut where you were open but the pass didn't come, or a strong boxout when the rebound bounces somewhere else.
Over a game, a player might stack up 20–40 of these microwins. Over a multi-game sample, a team’s micro-physicality profile starts to look like a fingerprint.
KD’s description isn’t exaggeration — it’s a direct match for the data. OKC creates a kind of two-way pressure loop: they love when you pressure them, and they double down by pressuring you even harder.
This shows up most clearly in the microwins — the constant hands, bumps, reroutes, and body contact that disrupt offensive flow without necessarily producing a steal. When you combine offense and defense, OKC generated +10 ball-pressure microwins per game over the sample.

One reason OKC’s profile is so powerful is that almost everything they’re doing on defense is teachable. They’re not relying on gimmicks. They’re winning with a mountain of small, repeatable habits – the same “microwins” tracked in this report.

Don’t just grade “stops.” Grade microwins. Show clips of reroutes, small bumps, and tiny disruptions that never show up in the boxscore. Make it clear to players that physicality isn’t binary – it accumulates over the course of a game.
One of OKC’s most repeatable edges: they attack the screen before it’s fully set. In the tagging, this shows up as bumps and contact on the screener as he’s arriving, which:
Shai, Dort, Wallace, and the bigs all contribute to these screen microwins.
Build in “touch the screener early” rules. Make every screen a small collision, not a clean setup. You don’t have to blow up the play every time—you just have to nudge the timing off rhythm.
OKC’s help rotations are aggressive but not reckless. The tags show a lot of collapse microwins (forced pickups, killed drives) without a spike in bad gambles or PH negatives.
The common thread: they react to the ball handler’s speed change, not just where he is on the floor.
Teach help rules based on pace triggers (when a driver turns the corner, gathers, or changes gears) rather than just “two feet in the paint.” That’s how you get aggressive help without living in scramble mode.

Opposing stars describe it as “they’re always in your space.” That’s not steal-hunting – it’s touch-hunting.
Track and reward touches on the ball and on the body—not just deflections and steals. Touches change angles, force re-dribbles, and slowly wear down primary creators.
In the “at rim” tags, Holmgren, Wallace, Dort and others pile up microwins simply by getting vertical early and presenting their chest. Blocks are nice; verticality wins are steady.
Grade contests on timing and body position, not just block attempts. A vertical chest that takes away the finish is a microwin; a late swipe is often a foul.

Stars and coaches keep saying the same thing: OKC is very physical without constantly fouling shooters. The tags back that up:
The Thunder concentrate their contact in moments where referees are least likely to blow the whistle, but the offense still feels it.
When you talk to players about being “more physical,” define where in the possession that should live: on the approach to the screen, on the first dribble, on the first cut – not on the shot release.
In the end, OKC isn’t just winning on size or length; they’re winning on sequencing. They apply force earlier, more often, and in smaller increments than almost anyone in the league. The good news for the rest of the NBA is that most of these edges are coachable.
